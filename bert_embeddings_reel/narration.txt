How does BERT turn ordinary text into vectors that actually understand context?

Let's take two sentences.

'I deposited money at the bank.'

And, 'I sat by the river bank.'

The word 'bank' appears in both, but clearly means different things.

Here's how BERT handles that.

First, it splits each sentence into smaller units called tokens. Some words remain whole, while others are broken into subwords.

Each token is mapped to a learned vector. BERT then adds positional information, and token-type information when applicable.

These vectors enter a stack of Transformer encoder layers.

Inside each layer, something interesting happens: bidirectional self-attention.

Every token can attend to other tokens on both sides of the sentence.

For example, 'bank' can attend to 'deposited' and 'money' in the first sentence.

But in the second, it can attend to 'river' and 'sat'.

Attention uses learned query, key, and value projections to calculate how information flows between tokens.

Across multiple attention heads and encoder layers, each token's representation is progressively updated.

So even though the original word is identical, BERT produces different contextual vectors for 'bank' in these two sentences.

But what if we need one vector for the entire sentence?

We can pool the token representations, for example by taking their mean. Another approach uses the final hidden state of the special CLS token.

However, vanilla BERT wasn't specifically trained to produce optimal sentence similarity embeddings.

That's why models like Sentence-BERT fine-tune the architecture to generate more useful sentence-level representations.

And that's the key idea: BERT doesn't simply look up a word's meaning. It constructs representations using the surrounding context.
